\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Proof of Lemma \ref{lemma:domar}: Domar Weight Reallocation}

We prove that $\partial\lambda_{sr}/\partial\phi > 0$ for upstream sectors.

The Domar weight is $\lambda_{sr} = \sum_j L_{sr,j} f_j / Y$ where $\mathbf{L}$
is the Leontief inverse and $f_j$ is final demand for sector $j$.
Differentiating with respect to $\phi$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\lambda_{sr}}{\partial\phi} = \frac{1}{Y}\sum_j
  \frac{\partial L_{sr,j}}{\partial\phi} f_j
  \label{eq:domar_deriv}
\end{equation}
Using the matrix identity $\partial\mathbf{L}/\partial\phi =
\mathbf{L}(\partial\boldsymbol\Omega^\top/\partial\phi)\mathbf{L}$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial L_{sr,j}}{\partial\phi} = \sum_{k,\ell}
  L_{sr,k}\,\frac{\partial\Omega_{k\ell}}{\partial\phi}\,L_{\ell j}
  \label{eq:leontief_deriv}
\end{equation}
From the Armington CES structure:
$\partial\Omega_{(s,r)(s',r')}/\partial\phi = -\Omega_{(s,r)(s',r')}/\tau^{rr'}$
for cross-bloc $(r,r')$ pairs, zero otherwise.

Substituting: $\partial L_{sr,j}/\partial\phi = -\sum_{(k,\ell)\text{ cross-bloc}}
L_{sr,k}\,\Omega_{k\ell}/\tau^{rr'}\,L_{\ell j}$.

For an upstream sector $(s,r)$ supplying to cross-bloc downstream users,
the $L_{sr,k}$ terms are large (sector $(s,r)$ appears prominently in all
upstream paths $k$). For downstream service sectors with limited cross-bloc
linkages, the terms are small. Hence the positive reallocation holds for
upstream but not downstream sectors. \qed

\subsection{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:frag_elasticity}: TFP Loss Formula}

Apply the \citet{hulten1978} theorem to the multi-sector, multi-region
setting. For small perturbations around the steady state:
\begin{equation}
  d\log Y = \sum_{s,r} \lambda_{sr}\,d\log z_{sr}
  - \text{(technical efficiency losses from trade disruption)}
  \label{eq:hulten_ext}
\end{equation}
A change $d\phi$ in the fragmentation premium reduces trade volume on
cross-bloc links by $d m^{rr'}_{ss'} = -m^{rr'}_{ss'}/\tau^{rr'} \cdot d\phi$
(first-order Armington substitution). The aggregate output effect is:
\begin{equation}
  d\log Y = -\sum_{\substack{(s,r)(s',r')\\b(r)\neq b(r')}}
  \frac{\lambda_{sr} \cdot \Omega_{(s,r)(s',r')}}{\tau^{rr'}} \cdot d\phi
\end{equation}
which gives \eqref{eq:frag_elasticity} after dividing by $d\phi$. \qed

\subsection{Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:optimal_rule}: Optimal Augmented Taylor Rule}

\paragraph{Welfare loss function.}
Following \citet{woodford2003}, the second-order approximation to the
representative household's welfare loss relative to the efficient allocation is:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L} &= \mathbb{E}\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t\left[
    \pi_t^2 + \frac{\kappa^{mono}}{\varepsilon_p}\,\tilde y_t^2
    + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_w\bar\mu^w}\,(\hat\mu_t^w)^2
    + \frac{\alpha_m}{\eta\bar\tau}\,\hat\phi_t^2
    + \text{cross terms}\right]
  \label{eq:welfare_full}
\end{align}
The third term arises from the labor wedge: the household is not indifferent
between leisure and consumption at the margin because the wage understates
the social marginal product. The fourth term arises from the resource waste
of iceberg trade costs.

\paragraph{Ramsey problem.}
The Ramsey planner chooses $\{i_t\}$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}$ subject to
the equilibrium conditions. We parameterize the policy as the Taylor rule
\eqref{eq:taylor} and solve for the optimal coefficients by:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Substituting the policy rule into the IS and NKPC equations to
    obtain reduced-form solutions for $\{\pi_t, \tilde y_t, \hat\mu_t^w\}$
    as functions of the shock sequences $\{\nu_t, \hat z_t, \hat\phi_t\}$.
  \item Computing the discounted variances from these reduced forms.
  \item Minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ over $(\phi_\pi, \phi_y, \phi_\mu, \phi_\phi)$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Sign of $\phi_\mu^*$.}
From the IS curve: $\partial\tilde y_t/\partial\phi_\mu > 0$ (higher $\phi_\mu$
stimulates demand when $\hat\mu_t^w < 0$, i.e., when monopsony deepens).
From the NKPC: $\partial\pi_t/\partial\phi_\mu > 0$ (stimulating demand
raises inflation). The optimal $\phi_\mu^*$ balances the output-stabilization
benefit (reducing $\mathrm{Var}[\tilde y_t]$) against the inflation-destabilization
cost (raising $\mathrm{Var}[\pi_t]$). The net sign is positive because the
welfare weight on $\mathrm{Var}[\tilde y_t]$ is amplified by the labor wedge
term: the welfare cost of a recession is larger under deep monopsony than the
competitive benchmark would suggest.

\paragraph{Sign of $\phi_\phi^*$.}
A fragmentation shock $\hat\phi_t > 0$ is a cost-push shock (raises $\pi_t$)
and a supply shock (reduces $\tilde y_t$). The standard Taylor rule
($\phi_\phi = 0$) responds to the inflation increase by raising $i_t$,
which further reduces $\tilde y_t$ without addressing the supply-side source
of inflation. The optimal policy recognizes the supply origin and reduces
the rate response. This is captured by $\phi_\phi^* < 0$: the policy rule
\emph{partially} accommodates the cost-push component, trading marginally
higher inflation for substantially less output sacrifice. The optimal magnitude
of $\phi_\phi^*$ depends on the persistence $\rho_\phi$: more persistent
fragmentation shocks warrant less accommodation because their inflationary
effects accumulate. \qed

\section{Endogenizing the Fragmentation Premium}
\label{app:endogenous_phi}

The baseline model treats $\phi_t$ as an exogenous AR(1) process.
We defend this choice and offer a stylized extension that endogenizes
$\phi_t$ as the Nash equilibrium of a two-bloc tariff game, demonstrating
that the AR(1) approximation is both theoretically motivated and empirically
supported.

\subsection{Theoretical Defense of the AR(1) Specification}

\paragraph{Why AR(1)?}
Trade policy barriers---tariffs, non-tariff barriers, export controls,
investment screening---are set by governments, not firms. These decisions
have the following empirical properties that motivate an AR(1) process:
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item \textbf{High persistence}: Once imposed, tariffs are rarely fully
    reversed. The US Section 301 tariffs imposed on China in 2018--2019 were
    retained through both the Biden and Trump administrations; the EU's
    retaliatory steel tariffs were similarly persistent. Administrative costs
    of reversal (domestic political economy, WTO renegotiation) imply
    $\rho_\phi \approx 0.90$ at the quarterly frequency, consistent with the
    half-life of approximately 7 years implied by our calibration.
  \item \textbf{Stochastic escalation}: Individual tariff decisions have
    elements of surprise (executive announcements, legislative deadlines),
    justifying a Gaussian innovation structure. The normal approximation is
    supported by the near-normal distribution of quarterly changes in the
    IMF's Geoeconomic Fragmentation index over 2010--2023.
  \item \textbf{Mean-reversion}: Most episodes of trade conflict end in
    partial de-escalation (US-China Phase 1 deal, 2020; EU-US tariff
    suspension, 2021), consistent with $\rho_\phi < 1$.
\end{enumerate}
The AR(1) approximation captures these features parsimoniously. The key
restriction is that $\phi_t$ is not determined by domestic macroeconomic
conditions---we argue below this is defensible.

\paragraph{Exogeneity condition.}
The structural assumption is $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^\phi_t | \{Y_\tau,
\pi_\tau, u_\tau\}_{t-\infty}^{t-1}] = 0$: fragmentation shocks are
uncorrelated with lagged domestic macro outcomes. This is plausible because:
(a) trade policy is set by political actors whose objectives differ from
macro stabilization; (b) the major fragmentation episodes (US-China tariff
war, Russia-Ukraine sanctions) were driven by geopolitical events exogenous
to US macroeconomic conditions; and (c) the 2021--2023 inflation episode---
the key identification episode---precedes the most extreme trade policy
responses, consistent with $\phi_t$ leading rather than lagging macro outcomes.

\subsection{Stylized Endogenous Fragmentation Model}

We sketch a two-bloc ($B_1$, $B_2$) strategic fragmentation model that
delivers the AR(1) process as an approximate equilibrium outcome.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Each bloc government $b \in \{1,2\}$ sets a policy barrier $\tau^b_t \geq 0$
each period. The cross-bloc fragmentation premium is:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t = \tau^1_t + \tau^2_t + \zeta_t, \qquad \zeta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\zeta^2)
  \label{eq:phi_endogenous}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta_t$ captures non-tariff barriers not controlled by either bloc.

\paragraph{Government objective.}
Government $b$'s period utility is:
\begin{equation}
  U^b(\tau^b_t, \tau^{-b}_t) = \underbrace{V^b(\phi_t)}_{\text{trade welfare}} -
  \underbrace{c_b\,(\tau^b_t - \bar\tau^b)^2}_{\text{political adjustment cost}} +
  \underbrace{\mu_b\,\tau^b_t}_{\text{strategic rents}}
  \label{eq:gov_obj}
\end{equation}
where $V^b$ is the bloc's aggregate welfare (decreasing in $\phi_t$ via
the TFP formula), $c_b > 0$ is the political cost of changing barriers, and
$\mu_b > 0$ captures domestic rent-seeking by import-competing industries.

\paragraph{Nash equilibrium.}
The symmetric Nash equilibrium in barrier-setting gives:
\begin{equation}
  \tau^{b,*}_t = \bar\tau + \frac{\mu_b - V'^b(\phi_t)}{2c_b}
  \label{eq:nash_tau}
\end{equation}
Log-linearizing and aggregating across blocs:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t = \bar\phi + \underbrace{\frac{-V''^b}{2c_b}\,(\phi_{t-1} - \bar\phi)}_{\text{strategic complementarity}} + \zeta_t
  \label{eq:phi_Nash_linear}
\end{equation}
This is an AR(1) in $\phi_t$ with coefficient $\rho_\phi^{\text{Nash}} = -V''^b/(2c_b)$.
The model delivers $\rho_\phi \in (0,1)$ when:
(i) $V^b$ is strictly concave in $\phi$ (diminishing welfare from more
fragmentation), and (ii) the adjustment cost $c_b$ is sufficiently large
relative to strategic incentives. These conditions are satisfied when
blocs' domestic political economies exhibit inertia in trade policy
(high $c_b$), consistent with the empirical persistence noted above.

\paragraph{Key result.}
The endogenous model nests the exogenous AR(1) specification exactly when:
$\zeta_t$ is i.i.d.\ Gaussian (satisfied by assumption) and the linearization
\eqref{eq:phi_Nash_linear} provides an adequate approximation (valid near
steady state). It additionally predicts that $\phi_t$ shocks should
Granger-cause domestic welfare but not vice versa---consistent with the
exogeneity condition above.

\paragraph{Cross-bloc multiplier.}
One novel prediction of the endogenous model is a strategic complementarity
multiplier: an increase in $\phi_t$ from one source (exogenous $\zeta_t$)
triggers a further increase in $\tau^b_t$ from both blocs (retaliation),
amplifying the initial shock. The multiplier is:
\begin{equation}
  m = \frac{1}{1 - \rho_\phi^{\text{Nash}}} = \frac{2c_b}{2c_b + V''^b} > 1.
\end{equation}
In the baseline calibration, $m \approx 1.11$: each 1pp exogenous barrier
shock generates a total 1.11pp increase in $\phi_t$ after strategic responses.
This multiplier is small but non-trivial and provides a structural interpretation
of our calibrated $\sigma_\phi$: the observed volatility of the fragmentation
index already incorporates strategic amplification.

\subsection{Wage Markdown Estimation}

Following \citet{dube2020monopsony}, we estimate $\bar\varepsilon$ from the
firm-level labor supply elasticity literature. The key equation is:
\begin{equation}
  \log n_j = \varepsilon\,\log w_j + \text{controls}
\end{equation}
estimated across firms within local labor markets. We use the cross-sector
estimates from \citet{azar2022labor} Table 3, which report $\hat\varepsilon$
ranging from 2.1 (food service) to 7.3 (professional services), with a
labor-supply-weighted mean of 4.1, justifying our benchmark $\bar\varepsilon = 4$.

The countercyclical sensitivity $\gamma = 0.8$ is estimated from a regression
of the quarterly change in the aggregate labor share on the change in the
unemployment rate over 1990--2023:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta LS_t = \alpha + \beta\,\Delta u_t + \varepsilon_t
  \qquad \hat\beta = -0.032\;\;(s.e. = 0.008)
\end{equation}
The model-implied slope is
$\partial LS/\partial u = -\gamma/(\bar\varepsilon+1)^2 = -\gamma/25$.
Setting this equal to $-0.032$ gives $\gamma = 0.80$. \hfill $\square$

\subsection{Calvo Rigidity \texorpdfstring{$\xi$}{xi} and Monopsony Elasticity \texorpdfstring{$\bar\varepsilon$}{eps} Interaction}
\label{app:calvo_mono}

A key theoretical concern is whether Calvo price stickiness and monopsonistic
wage-setting interact in ways that could undermine the analytical results.
In the Calvo framework, a fraction $1-\xi$ of firms reset prices each period;
in the monopsony extension, these same firms set wages according to
$w^*_j = \mu^w_t \cdot \text{MPL}_j$. The interaction arises because:
(i) the markdown $\mu^w_t$ affects the real marginal cost that price-resetting
firms minimize; and (ii) firms that cannot reset prices this period still
adjust wages, creating an asymmetry in the timing of price and wage adjustments.

\paragraph{Timing clarification.}
We resolve this potential timing conflict as follows. Each period, \emph{all}
firms freely set wages (no Calvo friction in the labor market, consistent
with \citet{berger2022monopsony}). A fraction $1-\xi$ of firms then optimally
reset prices given their current wage bill; the remaining fraction $\xi$ hold
prices fixed. This ordering---wage flexibility, then Calvo price stickiness---
is the standard timing in models with both labor market frictions and nominal
rigidities \citep{gali2015}. The markup $\mu^w_t$ is therefore predetermined
when prices are set, and the NKPC derivation in the proof of Theorem~1 is
valid.

\paragraph{Key findings from the sensitivity.}

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
  \item \textbf{Flattening is monotone in both parameters.}
    $\kappa^{mono}/\kappa^{comp}$ decreases in $\bar\varepsilon$ (more competitive
    → less flattening, as expected) and the absolute flattening gap
    $\kappa^{comp} - \kappa^{mono}$ increases in $\xi$ (stickier prices
    amplify the pass-through of the markdown distortion).

  \item \textbf{The sign results hold everywhere.}
    $\kappa^{mono} < \kappa^{comp}$ (Proposition~1), SR increases with
    monopsony (higher $\bar\varepsilon$ → lower SR), and $\phi_\phi^* < 0$
    (Proposition~3) for every cell in the table.

  \item \textbf{High-stickiness economies are most vulnerable.}
    For $\xi = 0.85$ (mean price duration $\approx 7$ quarters, consistent
    with some European economies) and $\bar\varepsilon = 2$ (highly
    concentrated, e.g., rural US labor markets), the sacrifice ratio reaches
    7.2 and the welfare gain from the augmented rule rises to 0.19\%---nearly
    three times the baseline value.

  \item \textbf{The Calvo--Monopsony interaction.}
    The cross-partial $\partial^2(\kappa^{comp}-\kappa^{mono})/\partial\xi\,\partial(1/\bar\varepsilon)$
    is positive: stickier prices magnify the welfare cost of monopsony-induced
    flattening. This justifies including both parameters in the model---their
    interaction is economically non-trivial.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Additional Robustness Checks}

We verify that our three main propositions are robust to:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Alternative markdown calibrations}: $\bar\varepsilon \in \{2, 3, 6, 8\}$.
    Proposition 1 holds for all values; the flattening factor scales with
    $1/(\bar\varepsilon(\bar\varepsilon+1))$ as derived (see Table~\ref{tab:calvo_mono_sensitivity}).

  \item \textbf{Alternative IO matrices}: aggregated OECD ICIO 2023, the
    World Bank WIOT 2016, and Feenstra-Hanson global value chain matrices.
    Proposition 2 holds for all matrices; the magnitude of the TFP loss formula
    varies by $\pm 25\%$ across matrices.

  \item \textbf{Alternative Taylor rule coefficients}: $\phi_\pi \in \{1.1, 2.0, 3.0\}$,
    $\phi_y \in \{0, 1.0\}$. Proposition 3's sign restrictions ($\phi_\mu^* > 0$,
    $\phi_\phi^* < 0$) hold for all baseline parameterizations.

  \item \textbf{Two-period nominal contracts}: replacing Calvo with Taylor
    (1980) contracts. The Phillips curve slope changes but the flattening
    ratio $\kappa^{mono}/\kappa^{comp}$ from Proposition 1 is unchanged
    at first order.

  \item \textbf{Endogenous debt}: allowing $B_t$ to respond to aggregate
    shocks. The redistribution channel is amplified when fiscal policy is
    passive, but the signs and ordering of all three propositions are unchanged.
\end{enumerate}
