\section{Quantitative Analysis}
\label{sec:quantitative}

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

We calibrate the model at a quarterly frequency. Table \ref{tab:calibration}
summarizes all structural parameters and their sources.

\paragraph{Household block.}
The discount factor $\beta = 0.985$ targets a quarterly real rate of 1.5\%,
consistent with pre-2022 long-run averages. The CRRA coefficient $\sigma = 2$
is standard. The inverse Frisch elasticity $\varphi = 2$ targets a Frisch
of 0.5, consistent with \citet{chetty2013}.

\paragraph{Nominal rigidity and goods market.}
The Calvo parameter $\xi = 0.75$ targets a mean price duration of four
quarters. The goods-market elasticity $\varepsilon_p = 6$ implies a price markup
of 20\%, in line with \citet{de2020rise}.

\paragraph{Monopsony block.}
We set $\bar\varepsilon = 4$, targeting the mean wage markdown of 0.80
documented by \citet{manning2021monopsony}. The countercyclical sensitivity
$\gamma = 0.8$ is estimated from the time-series relationship between
unemployment and the wage share documented in \citet{rinz2022labor}: a one
percentage point rise in unemployment reduces the labor share by approximately
$0.8 \times \gamma/(\bar\varepsilon+1)^2 = 0.032$ pp, consistent with the
data.

\paragraph{IO network.}
The domestic IO coefficient matrix $\boldsymbol\Omega_{domestic}$ is
calibrated to the BEA 2019 Use Table, aggregated from 71 to 10 sectors
following the mapping in the Supplemental Appendix. Regional trade shares
are from the IMF World Input-Output Database 2023.
The Armington elasticity $\eta = 2$ is the standard value from the trade
literature \citep{arkolakis2012}. The baseline within-bloc trade cost
$\delta^{within} = 0.05$ and cross-bloc premium $\bar\phi = 0.15$ are
calibrated to match the 2022 IMF Geoeconomic Fragmentation study's estimates
of intra-bloc trade intensity \citep{aiyar2023}.

\paragraph{Fragmentation shock process.}
The persistence $\rho_\phi = 0.90$ reflects the slow pace of supply chain
restructuring. The standard deviation $\sigma_\phi = 0.05$ is calibrated to
match the estimated variance of the bloc-trade intensity index over 2018--2023.

\subsection{Component Decomposition: Why Both Ingredients Are Necessary}
\label{sec:decomposition}

The core claim of the paper is that the IO$\times$Monopsony interaction
is non-reducible: neither channel alone generates the observed wage
dispersion and policy implications. We verify this in the full calibrated
model by comparing two nested variants:

\subsubsection{Channel Decomposition}
\label{sec:channel_decomp}

Table~\ref{tab:decomp} quantifies the analytical results of
Proposition~\ref{prop:toy}. \emph{IO alone}: the competitive model
generates uniform wage compression across sectors ($-1.4\%$ in both goods
and services) because without sector-heterogeneous monopsony, Domar
reallocation does not differentially affect wages. \emph{Monopsony alone}:
without IO-driven Domar reallocation, sector-specific markdowns deepen
proportionally to the aggregate unemployment response, generating no
goods/services dispersion. \emph{Joint}: Domar weight reallocation
concentrates activity in upstream sectors ($\varepsilon \approx 2.5$)
where markup deepening scales as $1/(\varepsilon+1)^2$, producing the
$3.4\times$ goods/services wage dispersion in row 5---matching the
analytical prediction from equation~\eqref{eq:core_nonlinearity}
exactly. The welfare gain from the augmented rule triples (from 0.01\%
to 0.03\%) because the joint model's stagflationary impulse is larger:
the central bank has more to gain by correctly identifying the supply-side
origin of inflation.

\subsection{Aggregate Validation: Model Moments vs.\ Data}
\label{sec:micro_moments}

Section~\ref{sec:empirical} establishes the CZ-level evidence using the Bartik
design. Here we verify that the calibrated model matches the key \emph{aggregate}
and \emph{sectoral} moments---again using predictions that are untargeted by
the calibration---providing a second, independent layer of validation. The
calibration targets (mean markdown, labor share cyclicality, trade bloc
intensity) are aggregate time-series moments; the cross-sectional wage and
Domar weight predictions below are model implications tested against
independent data sources.

\subsubsection{Prediction 1: Sectoral Real Wage Compression}

\paragraph{Model prediction.}
The IO$\times$Monopsony interaction predicts that sectors with high upstream
Domar weight \emph{and} high labor market concentration (low $\varepsilon_s$)
should exhibit the largest real wage compression during a fragmentation
episode. Define the predicted wage compression index for sector $s$:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_s^{\text{pred}} \equiv \Delta\lambda_s \cdot
  \frac{1}{(\varepsilon_s + 1)^2} \cdot \gamma
  \label{eq:wage_comp_index}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\lambda_s$ is the model-predicted Domar weight change under a
25pp fragmentation shock. Table~\ref{tab:micro_moments} compares model
predictions to BLS and BEA data for the 2021Q1--2023Q2 period.

\paragraph{Discussion.}
The model accounts for 91\% of cross-sectional variation in sectoral real
wage growth over 2021--2023, with no free parameters: the only free
aggregate parameter ($\Delta\phi = 0.25$) is calibrated to the aggregate
trade flow data, not the sectoral wage data. The model correctly ranks all
seven sectors and the predicted magnitudes are within 0.4 percentage points
of the data in every case. A model without the IO channel
(applying uniform Domar weight changes across sectors) has $R^2 = 0.12$;
a model without the monopsony channel (applying $\varepsilon_s = \bar\varepsilon = 4$
in all sectors) has $R^2 = 0.47$. The full model's $R^2 = 0.91$ confirms
that both channels are necessary.

\paragraph{Connection to the CZ regression.}
The sectoral pattern in Table~\ref{tab:micro_moments} is the sector-level
aggregation of the CZ-level regression in Proposition~\ref{prop:empirical}.
The wage compression index $\Phi_s^{\text{pred}}$ is the model's prediction
of the average within-sector cross-CZ pattern: sectors with higher
$\Phi_s^{\text{pred}}$ should also show larger $\hat\beta_3$ in the CZ
regression when that sector's employment share is the exposure measure.
The predicted aggregate $\hat\beta_3^{\text{model}} \approx -0.18$
(Section~\ref{sec:specification}) is consistent with the $R^2 = 0.91$
sector-level fit: both are driven by the $\Phi_s^{\text{pred}}$ index,
confirming that the Bartik design and the aggregate model are measuring
the same underlying IO$\times$Monopsony interaction at different levels
of aggregation.

\subsubsection{Prediction 2: Labor Share Dynamics by Concentration Quartile}

\paragraph{Model prediction.}
The countercyclical markdown implies that during tight-money episodes,
high-HHI sectors should see larger labor share declines. The model predicts
the labor share change for sector $s$:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta LS_s \approx -\frac{\gamma_s}{(\bar\varepsilon_s+1)^2}\,\Delta u_s
  \label{eq:ls_pred}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_s$ and $\bar\varepsilon_s$ are sector-specific parameters
from \citet{azar2022labor}.

\paragraph{Data.}
Using BEA sector-level value-added and compensation accounts over
2022Q2--2023Q4 (the Fed hiking cycle), we sort sectors into quartiles
by local-market HHI and compute the average change in the labor compensation
share. The model predicts a monotone relationship; the data show:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Bottom HHI quartile (least concentrated): $\Delta LS = -0.4\%$
    (model: $-0.3\%$)
  \item Top HHI quartile (most concentrated): $\Delta LS = -2.1\%$
    (model: $-1.9\%$)
\end{itemize}
The 5:1 ratio of labor share declines between the top and bottom quartile
is almost exactly what the structural formula \eqref{eq:ls_pred} predicts
using only the estimated $\varepsilon_s$ variation across sectors---again
an untargeted cross-sectional moment. A representative-agent model predicts
uniform labor share changes across concentration quartiles (ratio = 1:1).

\subsection{Exercise 1: The 2021--2023 Inflation Surge}
\label{sec:exercise1}

We test the model's ability to rationalize the post-2021 inflation episode
by simulating the sequence of supply shocks that occurred over 2021Q1--2023Q4:
semiconductor shortages (2021Q1--Q2), global shipping disruptions
(2021Q3--2022Q1), and the energy price shock associated with the Russia-Ukraine
conflict and accelerating geoeconomic fragmentation (2022Q1--2023Q2).

Figure \ref{fig:fig3} presents the impulse responses to a 25bp monetary
policy shock across three model variants: (i) the standard competitive NK
model with integrated trade, (ii) the monopsony-only model, and (iii) the
full model with monopsony and fragmentation.

\paragraph{Monetary policy transmission.}
Figure~\ref{fig:fig3} shows impulse responses to a 25bp monetary policy shock
across three model variants: the standard competitive NK model, the
monopsony-only model, and the full model with both monopsony and fragmentation.
The full model generates a more muted inflation response and a smaller output
trough than the competitive benchmark, consistent with the NKPC flattening
from Proposition~1. The monopsony channel dampens wage pass-through: when
employment rises during a rate-cut cycle, $\varepsilon_t$ rises, the markdown
$\mu_t^w$ falls, and wages rise by less than the marginal product, moderating
the inflationary pressure that would otherwise reverse the easing. This
wage-channel attenuation is absent from competitive NK models and accounts
for the $\kappa^{mono} - \kappa^{comp} = 0.004$ slope difference in the
calibrated NKPC.

\paragraph{Output resilience.}
The countercyclical markdown channel implies that when demand falls, firms
compress wages rather than cutting employment---maintaining headcount at the
cost of lower real wages. This mechanism provides a structural explanation
for the ``mysterious'' resilience of employment during 2022--2023 documented
by \citet{domash2022}: rather than cutting jobs, firms took larger markdowns,
consistent with the fall in the labour share of income documented in
\citet{rinz2022labor}.

\subsection{Exercise 2: A Tariff War Scenario}
\label{sec:exercise2}

We simulate a sudden 25pp increase in the cross-bloc fragmentation premium
$\phi_t$ (analogous to the 2018--2019 U.S.-China tariff escalation or a
hypothetical 2025+ scenario). Figure \ref{fig:fig4} shows the impulse
responses of output, inflation, import volume, and upstream concentration
under the standard vs.\ augmented Taylor rules.

\paragraph{Sectoral heterogeneity.}
Figure \ref{fig:fig5} shows the Domar weight reallocation. Energy, Mining,
and Basic Manufacturing see their Domar weights increase by 15--25\%
following the shock. Technology and Consumer Services see little change.
This reallocation is the mechanism behind the TFP loss: domestic upstream
sectors are less efficient (higher cost) substitutes for the disrupted
cross-bloc inputs.

\paragraph{Distributional consequences.}
Production workers in goods sectors (Energy, Mining, Manufacturing) face a
more dramatic markdown deepening than service workers: their firm-level
labor supply elasticity $\varepsilon$ is lower (estimated at 2.5 vs.\ 5.5
for service workers; see \citet{dube2020monopsony}), so the same increase
in $\phi_t$ compresses their wages more. In our calibration, a 25pp
fragmentation shock reduces real wages in goods sectors by 3.2\% vs.\ 1.4\%
for service sectors, implying a significant increase in inequality even in
the short run.

\paragraph{Policy comparison.}
Figure~\ref{fig:fig4} shows the fragmentation shock IRFs. Under the standard
Taylor rule, the central bank tightens in response to cost-push inflation,
deepening the output loss. Under the augmented rule ($\phi_\phi^* = -0.22$),
the rate response is more accommodative: the rule correctly identifies the
supply-side origin of the inflation and partially absorbs it, achieving a
smaller output trough with a modest inflation overshoot. The welfare gain from
this reallocation of the policy mix---0.03\% at baseline $\sigma_\phi = 0.05$,
rising to $\approx 0.5\%$ at $\sigma_\phi = 0.20$---is the quantitative
counterpart of Proposition~3.

\subsection{Cross-Country Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:exercise3}

The sacrifice ratio $1/\kappa^{mono}$ is increasing in monopsony severity
($\bar\varepsilon$) and decreasing in price flexibility ($1-\xi$)---the
Calvo$\,\times\,$monopsony sensitivity table in the Supplemental Appendix
shows this robustness in detail. Countries with higher labor market concentration face flatter
Phillips curves and require larger rate increases per unit disinflation.
Figure~\ref{fig:fig6} plots the model-implied sacrifice ratio against
country-level HHI: the US (HHI $\approx 4{,}000$, $\bar\varepsilon \approx 3$)
requires modestly more tightening than the Euro Area (HHI $\approx 2{,}800$,
$\bar\varepsilon \approx 4$) for the same disinflation, consistent with the
qualitative pattern of the 2022--2024 tightening cycle. We caution that
cross-country differences in $\bar\varepsilon$ are imprecisely estimated;
the model provides a structural lens, not a precise cross-country prediction.
