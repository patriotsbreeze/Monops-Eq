Consider two commuting zones in 2022: a rural Appalachian mining county and a
suburban professional services corridor. Both face the same Federal Reserve
rate hikes. But only the mining county---heavily employed in upstream sectors
dependent on cross-bloc intermediate inputs, with a labor market HHI above
4{,}000---sees real wages fall by 3\% over 2022--2023. The services corridor
sees wages fall by less than 1\%. Standard monetary models predict the same
rate-hike transmission for both. This paper builds and estimates a model that
predicts the difference.

The core mechanism is a non-linear interaction that requires two simultaneous
ingredients: a multi-region input-output network (the mining county's upstream
exposure to fragmented supply chains) and structural monopsony (the concentrated
labor market that amplifies wage compression). Remove either ingredient, and the
differential wage response disappears. We document this interaction in
commuting-zone microdata, then build the framework needed to understand its
aggregate and distributional consequences.

\paragraph{The Monopsony Fact.}
\citet{azar2022labor}, \citet{rinz2022labor}, and \citet{benmelech2022}
document that the median U.S.\ worker faces a local labor market
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) exceeding 4{,}000---the Department of
Justice's threshold for ``highly concentrated.'' Wage markdowns (the gap
between the marginal product of labor and the observed wage) average 15--30\%
across sectors, with substantial cross-sectional variation positively
correlated with HHI \citep{azar2022labor}. Critically, this gap is
\emph{countercyclical}: firms compress wages more aggressively in downturns,
when workers' outside options deteriorate and firm-level labor supply
elasticities fall \citep{dube2020monopsony, manning2021monopsony}. This
decouples unemployment from inflation in ways standard models cannot reproduce,
and it implies that the aggregate Phillips curve is \emph{endogenously
flatter} than competition-based calibrations suggest.

\paragraph{The Fragmentation Fact.}
The IMF's Geoeconomic Fragmentation series \citep{aiyar2023} documents a
measurable bifurcation of global trade into politically aligned blocs,
accelerating sharply post-2022. Using bilateral trade flow data, \citet{gopinath2024}
show that trade within Western-aligned and China-aligned blocs has remained
resilient, while cross-bloc trade has been rerouted through costly
intermediary countries or reshored domestically. This structural shift means
supply shocks that were previously absorbed by flexible global substitution
are now amplified domestically and propagate through concentrated upstream
networks with elevated Domar weights \citep{baqaee2019}.

\paragraph{The Critical Non-Linearity.}
These two facts interact in a way that \emph{cannot} be captured by studying
them in isolation---and this interaction is the central contribution of the
paper. Trade fragmentation shifts production toward domestic upstream sectors:
Energy, Mining, and Basic Manufacturing absorb the largest Domar weight
increases when cross-bloc trade costs rise. These same sectors are precisely
where monopsony is most severe. Firm-level labor supply elasticities in goods
production average $\varepsilon \approx 2.5$, less than half the service-sector
average of $5.5$ \citep{dube2020monopsony}. Because the wage markdown deepens
as $1/(\varepsilon+1)^2$, a fragmentation shock that reallocates activity
toward high-monopsony sectors triggers wage compression that is approximately
$[(5.5+1)^2/(2.5+1)^2] \approx 3.4$ times larger per unit Domar weight
reallocation in goods than in services. In our calibration, a 25pp tariff
shock reduces real wages in goods sectors by 3.2\% versus 1.4\% in services.
An IO model without monopsony misses this dispersion entirely (it predicts
proportional wage effects). A monopsony model without IO misses the
concentration of Domar weight in high-monopsony sectors and
therefore understates the aggregate wage compression by a factor of
two. Only the joint model correctly identifies the distributional incidence of
fragmentation shocks and, as a consequence, the correct augmented Taylor rule
response.

\paragraph{The Central Gap.}
No existing general equilibrium model integrates both of these facts in a
dynamic, quantitative framework. IO network models \citep{baqaee2019,
acemoglu2012} embed input-output linkages into NK frameworks but assume
competitive labor markets and integrated trade. Monopsony has been grafted
onto static or partial-equilibrium NK models \citep{berger2022monopsony,
autor2020fall}, but without the multi-region network structure that determines
which sectors absorb fragmentation shocks. This paper fills that gap.

\paragraph{What This Paper Does.}

We build a model with two novel, interacting components:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Structural monopsony with a countercyclical markdown.}
  Firms face upward-sloping firm-level labor supply curves, parameterized by
  a firm-level elasticity $\varepsilon$ that falls when unemployment rises.
  The resulting wage markdown $\mu^w = \varepsilon/(\varepsilon+1)$ deepens in
  recessions, generating endogenous, state-dependent Phillips curve flattening.
  Section~\ref{sec:toy_model} derives this mechanism in closed form in a
  two-sector analytical model before the full calibration.

\item \textbf{Multi-region, multi-bloc IO network.}
  The world is partitioned into geopolitical blocs. Trade costs between blocs
  are higher and subject to a time-varying \emph{fragmentation premium} $\phi_t$
  that follows an AR(1) process with high persistence. Firms source intermediates
  using a nested CES structure over domestic and foreign varieties; the
  Armington elasticity $\eta$ governs substitution across regions. We represent
  the resulting multi-sector production structure as a weighted directed graph
  and compute Domar weights and Leontief inverses analytically.
\end{enumerate}

We solve the model by standard log-linearization around the non-stochastic
steady state, extended with two novel closed-form blocks: the
\emph{monopsony block}, which adds the countercyclical markdown response
to the standard wage-employment system; and the \emph{fragmentation block},
which maps shocks to the trade cost matrix $\boldsymbol{\tau}(\phi_t)$ into
shocks to the Leontief inverse. Both blocks are analytically tractable, which
is why we can state Propositions~1--3 as pencil-and-paper results rather than
computational simulations.

\paragraph{Main Results.}

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (Phillips Curve Flattening).}
The monopsony-adjusted NKPC slope is:
\begin{equation*}
  \kappa^{mono} = \kappa_{mc}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma} + \frac{\varphi}{\alpha_n}
    - \frac{\gamma}{(\bar\varepsilon+1)^2(1-\bar u)\,\alpha_n}\right)
  < \kappa^{comp},
\end{equation*}
where $\kappa_{mc} = (1-\xi)(1-\beta\xi)/\xi$ is the Calvo coefficient on
real marginal cost. The second term in the bracket---absent in the competitive
model---reflects the countercyclical markdown channel: when employment rises,
firm-level labour supply becomes more elastic ($\varepsilon_t$ rises), the
markdown falls, and the wage rises by less than the marginal product, damping
marginal cost pass-through to prices. In our baseline calibration
($\bar\varepsilon = 4$, $\gamma = 0.8$, $\alpha_n = 0.65$), the flattening is
1.4\% ($\kappa^{mono} = 0.307$, $\kappa^{comp} = 0.311$). The mechanism is
qualitatively robust across parameterisations; quantitative magnitudes scale with
$\gamma$ and rise substantially for lower values of $\bar\varepsilon$ (higher
monopsony concentration).

\paragraph{Proposition 2 (Fragmentation Amplification).}
The semi-elasticity of aggregate TFP with respect to the fragmentation premium
is:
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d\log\mathrm{TFP}}{d\phi} = -\!\!\!\sum_{\substack{(s,r),(s',r')\\b(r)\neq b(r')}}
    \lambda_{sr} \,\Omega_{(s,r)(s',r')} \,\frac{1}{\tau^{rr'}},
\end{equation*}
a closed-form, network-weighted expression. This quantity is strictly negative
and increasing in magnitude in the centrality of cross-bloc linkages. In our
calibrated 10-sector, 5-region model with the 2019 BEA Use Table, a 15pp
increase in $\phi$ reduces aggregate TFP by 0.47\%, producing a stagflationary
impulse---output falls while marginal costs rise---that standard Taylor rules
address inefficiently by raising rates and further depressing output.

\paragraph{Proposition 3 (Optimal Augmented Taylor Rule).}
The welfare-optimal monetary rule is:
\begin{equation*}
  i_t = r^* + \pi^* + \phi_\pi^*(\pi_t - \pi^*) + \phi_y^*\,\tilde{y}_t
        + \underbrace{\phi_\mu^*\,\hat\mu_t^w}_{\text{novel: }>0}
        + \underbrace{\phi_\phi^*\,\hat\phi_t}_{\text{novel: }<0} + \nu_t.
\end{equation*}
The sign of $\phi_\mu^* \geq 0$: monopsony deepening widens the labor
wedge below the competitive level; the central bank partially offsets
the wage compression through accommodation.
The sign of $\phi_\phi^* < 0$:
fragmentation spikes are supply shocks---standard Taylor rules tighten into
the output loss; the augmented rule ($\phi_\phi^* = -0.22$) trades a modest
inflation overshoot for substantially less output sacrifice.

\textit{Relation to the literature.}
We build on three bodies of work. On New Keynesian models: \citet{clarida1999}
and \citet{woodford2003} establish the standard three-equation framework;
we add structural monopsony and IO fragmentation as two analytically tractable
extensions with first-order policy implications. On IO networks:
\citet{baqaee2019} and \citet{acemoglu2012} price sectoral shocks via Domar
weights; we extend this to a multi-bloc setting where the Leontief inverse
varies with geopolitical fragmentation. On labor market power:
\citet{berger2022monopsony} introduce monopsony into a static NK model;
we are the first to embed countercyclical monopsony in a dynamic IO network
framework and derive its implications for optimal monetary policy, including
the closed-form characterization of the augmented Taylor rule.
